MISSION 03 / Reading

Your brain is not a camera

Read the article, then explain it in your own words.

11 min
SCIENCE NOTES / 01
✦ Green words are interactive — hover to see the Russian translation, or tap on mobile.

Imagine two friends talking about the same birthday party. One remembers loud music and a blue cake. The other is sure the cake was green and the music was quiet. Are they both lying?

We often think of memory as a video that we can watch again. However, scientists say that remembering is more like a story. Our brains keep important information, but they may not keep every small detail. When we remember something, we use both the original experience and information we learned later.

Psychologist Elizabeth Loftus studied how questions can influence people's memories. In a famous experiment about car accidents, people watched a video and answered questions about what they had seen. Changing one word in a question affected the answers. This is one example of the .

Another type of experiment asks people to remember a list of connected words. Participants may remember a very related word that was never shown. This does not mean their brains are broken. It shows that memory often keeps the , not only exact details.

So, can we trust our memories? Often, yes. Memories help us learn and make decisions every day. But when an exact detail matters, it is wise to look for more , especially if someone asks a leading question.

MISSION 05 / B1 Grammar

How certain are you?

Make deductions with must, might, could and can't.

10 min
Very likely
must be

“She must be tired. She worked all night.”

Possible
might / could be

“He might be at home, but I'm not sure.”

Not possible
can't be

“That can't be Anna. She's abroad.”

Form: modal + base verb. Use must be for strong positive deductions, can't be for strong negative deductions. Might be / could be express possibility — they are not proof.

Make the best deduction
Speak: Think of a situation: “My friend isn't replying to her messages.” Give three possible explanations with must, might and can't where the evidence supports them.

MISSION 10 / Reading

When seeing isn't believing

Deepfakes, misleading images, and how to verify stories.

10 min
DIGITAL LIFE / 02
✦ Green words are interactive — hover to see the Russian translation, or tap on mobile.

Have you ever watched a video online and thought, “That can't be real”? Today, it can be difficult to know whether a video is real, edited or created by artificial intelligence.

A is a video, image or sound recording that uses technology to make something look or sound real when it isn't. For example, a video might show a famous person saying words they never said. Not every AI-generated video is harmful, but fake media can be used to trick people.

Sometimes, a picture is completely real but its is misleading. An old photograph may be shared as if it were taken yesterday. A video can also be cut so we only see one part of an event. In these cases, the problem is not always the image itself, but the story around it.

How can we check information? First, before sharing a surprising claim. Next, find out who originally published it. Is the source reliable? Then look for the same information in other trustworthy places. If you can, check the original photo or video and its date.

Remember: a confident voice, many likes and a realistic picture do not prove a story is true. It is better to say than to share something that might be false.

MISSION 11 / B1 Grammar

What did they actually say?

Reported speech: statements and questions.

12 min
DIRECT → REPORTED

“I saw someone in red.”

Maya said (that) she had seen someone in red.

“The poster is missing.”

He said (that) the poster was missing.

SAY / TELL / ASK

said (that) + sentence

told me (that) + sentence

asked if / whether + statement word order

“Did you see a bag?” → She asked if I had seen a bag.

Useful changes
am/is → was
are → were
can → could
will → would
have/has seen → had seen
see/saw → saw/had seen
today → that day
yesterday → the day before
tomorrow → the next day

We usually shift tenses when reporting something later in the past. If a fact is still true or we report it immediately, a shift may not be necessary.

Report the witness statements
Speaking game: The teacher gives a new statement. Report it immediately: “The witness said that...” Then swap roles.

HOMEWORK / 15–20 min

Writing: A memory that changed

Write 120–150 words about a time when you and another person remembered the same event differently. Describe both versions and say what evidence could help.

Use: At first I thought… / She said that… / I might have… / Later we discovered…

Critical thinking: Fact-check a claim

Find a harmless surprising claim online. Write its source, what evidence is offered, what another trustworthy source says, and your conclusion (80–100 words).

If you cannot verify the claim, write unverified — do not call it false without evidence.
